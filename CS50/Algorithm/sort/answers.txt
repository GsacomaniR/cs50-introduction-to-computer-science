How do you know?: The timings show a clear difference between the best case (sorted: 0.985s for 50,000 elements) and the worst cases (random: 6.064s and reversed: 4.504s for 50,000 elements).
The increase from 0.038s for 5,000 elements to 0.985s for 50,000 elements in sorted lists (a ~25× factor) suggests a best-case time complexity of O(n), while the increase from 0.052s to
6.064s in random lists (a ~116× factor) indicates a worst-case time complexity of O(n²). This pattern of performance varying according to the initial ordering is characteristic of
bubble sort, especially when implemented with the optimization that stops early if no swaps are made.

How do you know?: The timings are very similar in all cases: random (2.811s), reversed (2.905s), and sorted (2.784s) for 50,000 elements. The increase from approximately 0.045s for
5,000 elements to 2.8s for 50,000 elements (a ~60–70× factor for 10× more elements) indicates O(n²) time complexity.

Unlike bubble sort, selection sort does not benefit from partially sorted lists, since it always scans the entire array to find the smallest element, which explains the consistency of
the timings regardless of the initial ordering. Additionally, it is slower than merge sort for large lists (2.8s vs. 0.9s for 50,000 elements), as expected for an O(n²) algorithm.
